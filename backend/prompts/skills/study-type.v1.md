Work out what kind of study the paper is before judging any of it.

1. State its aim in one or two sentences, in the authors' terms, then in plain words.
2. Name the kind of study: for example a new method or tool, an experiment comparing approaches, a benchmark, a survey or review, a case study, an interview or questionnaire study, a replication, or a position paper. If it mixes kinds, say which part is which.
3. If a language model is part of how the study was done (not only what it studies), name its role: labelling or coding data, rating or judging outputs, summarising or synthesising sources, standing in for human participants, being studied as a tool people use, being built into a new tool, or being benchmarked. If no language model is involved, say so in one line and move on.
4. Say what kind of evidence this kind of study can give, and what it cannot: an experiment can show a difference under its conditions, a case study can show that something happens, a survey can show what people report.
5. Close with the two or three things most worth checking before believing the main result, in plain words: for example how they evaluated it, where their ground truth came from, what they left out, or whether someone could repeat it. Point to where in the paper each one is decided.

If the paper's aim or kind is genuinely unclear from the text, say that, and say what is missing.
