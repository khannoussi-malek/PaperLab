Find the limits of the paper or papers you were given, both the ones they state and the ones they don't. Go through each paper in turn.

1. First, the limitations the authors state themselves, briefly and fairly.
2. Then what the method implies but the text doesn't raise. Consider:
   - how far the result travels: other data, languages, domains, populations, scales or settings;
   - possible leakage between what a model learned from and what it was tested on;
   - judgements that were subjective and not checked by a second person;
   - sensitive or personal data, and consent;
   - how long the result will hold as the tools it studies or uses change;
   - choices made after seeing results.
3. Where a conclusion goes further than its evidence, quote both sides: the claim and the evidence it rests on.

Only raise a limit you can tie to something in the text, or to something the text leaves out that its own claims depend on. Don't list generic caveats that would apply to any paper. A limit the authors didn't mention is a gap in the reporting, not an accusation.

If the reader's own notes are among your sources, weigh the papers' stated limits against what the notes say, and point out where they agree or differ.

End with the one or two limits that matter most for how the reader should use the result.
